## 2. Why this is not an embedding problem

> **Expected.** The failure above looks like a weak encoder. It is not; it is arithmetic. For L2-normalised pooled vectors the cosine is the shared mass: if two texts differ in a feature set D, the similarity drop is the share of the vector's squared weight carried by D. So swapping *germany* for *france* costs exactly what *germany* is worth in that sentence, and no more. **Two predictions:** (a) the measured drop tracks the differing-feature mass share closely, and (b) because a paraphrase rewrites more of the sentence than a decisive swap does, the most similar confusable pairs will score ABOVE a large share of the genuine paraphrases. If (b) holds, no threshold can work, and that is a theorem about pooling rather than a complaint about TF-IDF.

### The crux

| quantity                                                                                             | value      |
|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------------|
| 90th percentile of CONFUSABLE similarity                                                             | 0.498      |
| paraphrase pairs scoring BELOW it                                                                    | 156 of 196 |
| i.e. share of genuine paraphrases a threshold set to reject the worst 10% of traps would also reject | 79.6%      |


**Found.** (b) A threshold high enough to reject the top decile of confusable pairs throws away 79.6% of genuine paraphrases. **The orderings interleave, so the populations are not separable by any cut of this score.** That is not a defect of TF-IDF. Any encoder that reduces a sentence to one vector spends its capacity on the sentence's bulk, and the decisive word is not the bulk. A better encoder changes the numbers; it does not change the shape. The fix has to live outside the similarity function.


## 3. The aggregate false-hit rate measures your traffic, not your cache

> **Expected.** Exact matching should hit often - 4000 requests over 163 surface forms - and be incapable of a wrong answer. Semantic matching should hit more. The prediction is the one from section 1, stated operationally: **hold the cache configuration fixed and change only the traffic mix, and the headline false-hit rate will move a lot while the per-subset error rates barely move.** If that happens, the aggregate is a property of the workload and cannot be used to accept or reject a cache.

## 4. The fix: a veto that lives outside the similarity function

> **Expected.** Section 2 says a decisive token cannot dominate a pooled vector, so stop asking it to. Extract each query's decisive tokens and veto a hit when they disagree, regardless of cosine. The obvious definition of decisive is *high IDF*. The prediction was that an IDF cut would work. **It does not, and the reason is worth more than the fix.**

| 70.0%          | 5.449 |

Over 170 documents most of the vocabulary occurs once or twice, so the IDF distribution is a spike. Every percentile from the 10th to the 90th returns the same cut, which means *there is no IDF threshold to tune*: any setting admits nearly every content word - `but`, `cannot`, `answer` alongside `germany` and `401`. The decisive set then grows with sentence length, which reintroduces exactly the dilution the guard was built to escape. This is a corpus-size artefact, and it is also what every team hits when they compute IDF over their own small document set instead of a background corpus.


### Bound the size of the set instead

Take the K highest-IDF tokens of each query, however common they are. K is a budget, not a threshold, so it is insensitive to the shape of the IDF distribution and to sentence length. Two queries about different countries cannot agree on their top-K; two phrasings of the same question usually can.

This is section 3's error committed against the fix: a number dominated by benign traffic, quoted as if it described the hard case.

Two structural notes visible in the table. At K=1 the Jaccard parameter is inert: one token gives a Jaccard of 0 or 1, so every setting in (0,1] decides identically. And J does not mean the same thing at different K, because the union grows with K - which is why the sweep is not monotone in K at fixed J. The parameter with a stable meaning is *how many of the K tokens must agree*, not the ratio.


## 5. Tenant scoping is not free, and the bill is worth paying

> **Expected.** Twenty of the intents are platform-wide - status, policy, protocol - and their answer is the same for every customer, so an unscoped cache reuses them across tenants and gets a higher hit rate. The other intents are the customer's own contract. Three intents are worded IDENTICALLY across three tenants and have three different answers; no similarity function can separate them, because they are not semantically different. The prediction: unscoped wins on hit rate and leaks; scoped loses hit rate and cannot leak.

| policy           | hit rate | entries | false-hit rate | wrong answers on tenant-specific intents | of which the identical-wording trap |
|------------------|----------|---------|----------------|------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------|
| unscoped         | 93.4%    | 137     | 6.08%          | 185                                      | 96                                  |
| scoped by tenant | 90.6%    | 241     | 3.31%          | 89                                       | 0                                   |


**Found.** Scoping costs 2.8% of hit rate and 104 extra entries, because every platform-wide answer is now cached once per tenant. It is still the only correct default. The identical-wording trap is the proof: three tenants ask *"what is my monthly api request limit"* in the same words and need three different answers. There is no threshold, no guard and no encoder that separates byte-identical strings. **Isolation has to be structural, because it is the one property similarity cannot express.**


## 6. The wrong answer your cache metrics cannot see

> **Expected.** Coalescing merges concurrent duplicate requests into one backend call. Merging on identical text is safe. Merging on SIMILARITY is a semantic cache hit under a different name - except that the cache is never asked, so when it serves the wrong answer, no cache counter moves. The prediction: semantic coalescing raises the hit rate, produces wrong answers, and leaves cache-reported precision untouched.

| policy                      | hit rate | backend calls | coalesced | cache false hits | coalesced wrong | poisoned entries | precision AS REPORTED BY THE CACHE | true precision |
|-----------------------------|----------|---------------|-----------|------------------|-----------------|------------------|------------------------------------|----------------|
| no coalescing               | 90.6%    | 377           | 0         | 120              | 0               | 0                | 96.7%                              | 96.7%          |
| exact coalescing            | 94.0%    | 241           | 136       | 120              | 0               | 0                | 96.7%                              | 96.8%          |

| semantic + cache the result | 94.5%    | 221           | 158       | 162              | 3               | 3                | 95.5%                              | 95.6%          |


### The knob operators actually turn

The cache threshold is scrutinised because everyone knows a cache can serve a stale or wrong answer. The coalescing threshold is not, because coalescing is filed under *performance*. The reasoning goes: it is one request, it is in-flight anyway, and merging saves a whole backend call - so set it loose. Here is that reasoning, priced. The cache configuration is fixed at 0.60 throughout; only the coalescing threshold moves.

| coalescing threshold | backend calls | coalesced | coalesced WRONG | precision of the coalescer | precision the cache reports |
|----------------------|---------------|-----------|-----------------|----------------------------|-----------------------------|
| 0.800                | 233           | 146       | 0               | 100.0%                     | 96.7%                       |
| 0.700                | 230           | 149       | 0               | 100.0%                     | 96.7%                       |
| 0.600                | 221           | 158       | 3               | 98.1%                      | 95.5%                       |
| 0.500                | 220           | 164       | 6               | 96.3%                      | 95.5%                       |
| 0.400                | 219           | 179       | 9               | 95.0%                      | 95.5%                       |
| 0.300                | 219           | 197       | 10              | 94.9%                      | 95.5%                       |


**Found.** At the default 0.60, semantic coalescing saved 156 backend calls and served 3 wrong answers to do it. Loosen the knob and the coalescer's own precision falls away while **the number on the cache dashboard barely moves, because every one of those requests was a cache MISS** - the cache was asked, answered honestly, and is not implicated. Caching the coalesced answer, which is what a naive implementation does because it has a query and an answer in hand, converts 3 transient mistakes into permanent entries. The general form: **any component that can return another request's answer is a cache and needs a cache's metrics**, whatever the package it lives in.


### The concurrency half, for real

The policy above is measured on a virtual clock so the numbers are reproducible. The mechanism is a real streaming single-flight (`internal/flight`) and is exercised as one. Here it is under an actual herd of goroutines:

```
33 concurrent requests for the same question
  backend calls            : 1
  leaders                  : 1
  followers                : 32
  late joiners (mid-stream): 1
  distinct bodies received : 1
    33 x "the refund window is 30 days"
  leaders + followers == requests: true
```

Two properties there are not the textbook ones. The work is owned by the GROUP, not by the first caller, so the leader abandoning does not fail everyone waiting behind it - and under a herd the first caller is precisely the one who has been waiting longest and is likeliest to have given up. And a subscriber that arrives mid-stream is replayed the prefix, because a late joiner silently receiving a truncated answer is worse than an error.


## 7. What to take away

1. **An aggregate false-hit rate on a semantic cache is close to meaningless.** 97% of query pairs are trivially unrelated, so the aggregate is dominated by the easy case. Report the error rate on the confusable subset or report nothing.

2. **The failure is structural, not a weak encoder.** Pooling bounds how far one token can move the vector. Swapping *germany* for *france* cannot cost more similarity than *germany*'s share of the sentence's mass, and that share is small. A better encoder shifts the numbers; the bound survives.

3. **So fix it outside the similarity function.** A rare-token veto is length-insensitive by construction and can overrule a high cosine. This is the transferable idea: when one feature must be able to veto a decision, it cannot be a term in a weighted mean.

4. **Coalescing is a cache, and it is a cache with no metrics.** Every semantic merge of two in-flight requests is a cache hit that the cache never sees and never counts. If you coalesce on similarity, your false-hit dashboard is measuring a subset of your false hits and telling you it is all of them.

5. **Isolation is the one property similarity cannot express.** Three tenants asking a byte-identical question and needing three different answers is not a hard NLP problem; it is a problem that NLP is the wrong tool for. Scope structurally and pay the hit rate.


## 8. What this does not show

See `docs/known-limitations.md`. The three that most affect the numbers above: the similarity function is a TF-IDF cosine over a hand-authored synonymy table, not a transformer; the corpus is 170 queries, so tail percentiles are coarse; and the adversarial intents are boosted 2x in the arrival stream, which inflates every error rate relative to a benign production stream. The *comparisons* are unaffected - both arms of every comparison see the same stream - but the absolute error rates are a worst case, not a forecast.
