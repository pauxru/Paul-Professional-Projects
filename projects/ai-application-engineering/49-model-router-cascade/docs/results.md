1. IS THE CHEAP MODEL'S CONFIDENCE WORTH ANYTHING?
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A cascade escalates when the small model is unsure. Before building one,
measure whether "unsure" means anything. Two separate questions:
  (a) does the signal RANK correctly?  -> AUC
  (b) are its NUMBERS true?            -> ECE
A signal can pass (a) and fail (b), and most real ones do.

policy                        cost   accuracy         p95    vs random  cost saved
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------
always-mid                    675¢      68.5%       636ms    +19.3 pts        +74%

always-mid sits +19.3 points ABOVE the chord. The fleet's cost/quality curve
is convex, so a mixture of small and mid beats a mixture of small and large
at every budget in between - with no routing logic whatsoever.

The correct zero-information baseline is the upper convex hull of the fixed
policies, which random mixing attains at any budget:

  hull vertex          cost   accuracy

  always-small          45      43.0%

Everything from here is scored against that hull. This matters more than it
sounds: "we beat random escalation to the frontier model" and "we are worse
than just using the mid-tier model" can both be true at once, and a team can
ship a router that loses money while celebrating the first one.

4. THE ADVICE THAT DOES NOTHING: RECALIBRATE THE CONFIDENCE
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Section 1 found the confidence signal badly calibrated. Standard advice: fit
a temperature and the cascade improves. So fit one - on the TRAINING split
only - and re-run the identical sweep.

Fitted temperature: 1.59. The simulator's small-model Calibration is 1.90,
so the fit recovered the truth to within 0.31 - the machinery works.

confidence signal                 ECE        MCE      Brier        AUC
----------------------------------------------------------------------
raw (as reported)              0.0588     0.2263     0.1198     0.9177
temperature-scaled             0.0288     0.1467     0.1151     0.9177

Calibration error fell 51%. Brier improved. AUC moved from 0.9177 to 0.9177.
First clue. Now the frontier:

policy                          cost   accuracy  escalated   vs baseline   cost saved
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
cascade-s>l@0.91               3732¢      86.2%        78%      +2.5 pts         +12%
recal-s>l@0.75                 3578¢      85.4%        73%      +2.5 pts         +12%

Pareto frontier, raw cascade:    48 points, best accuracy 0.8738, best lift +2.48
Pareto frontier, scaled cascade: 48 points, best accuracy 0.8748, best lift +2.47

The two sweeps land on different THRESHOLDS, so comparing the grids point
for point proves nothing. The right check pairs each scaled threshold T with
the raw threshold temper(T, 1/t) it is supposed to equal, and asks whether
the two policies make identical decisions:

  48 threshold pairs checked; 48 agreed on every one of the 4000 queries;
  largest disagreement across all pairs: 0 queries

The frontier does not move. Not by a basis point. A threshold cascade only
ever asks `confidence >= T`, and temperature scaling is MONOTONE, so it
cannot change WHICH queries clear the bar - only which number on the dial
corresponds to that set. A worked sample of those pairs:

scaled T    identical raw T    escalation    accuracy       cost same?
----------------------------------------------------------------------------
0.30        0.2063                  48.5%      0.7498       2505 true
0.50        0.5000                  58.0%      0.8035       2888 true
0.70        0.7937                  69.9%      0.8452       3453 true
0.90        0.9705                  87.6%      0.8710       4028 true

TestRecalibrationIsAReparameterisation asserts this per query, not just in
aggregate. What recalibration DOES buy is that the dial means something:
on the raw signal "escalate below 0.90" escalates 78% of traffic; on the
scaled signal it escalates 88%, and those really are the queries the small
model gets right less than 90% of the time. You can set that knob from a
product requirement instead of tuning it against a spreadsheet. That is
worth doing. It is not a quality win, and reporting it as one is how a team
spends a quarter on calibration and ships an unchanged product.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. THE FIX NOBODY MENTIONS: PRICE THE DECISION
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Section 0 measured a 104x spread in prompt size, and prompt size explains
only 10% of the variance in difficulty. A fixed threshold ignores both: it
spends the same confidence budget on a 44-token query and a 4567-token one,
though escalating the second costs 104x more and is barely more likely to
need it. The question was never "am I unsure enough" - it is "is what I
would learn worth what it costs".

  escalate when  (P(large right) - confidence) / marginal_cost  >=  lambda

Lambda is accuracy points per cent: a price, not a knob. And note what
changed - the confidence is now SUBTRACTED, not compared. Arithmetic is not
monotone-invariant, so unlike section 4 this rule CAN be affected by
recalibration. Both variants are measured below.

policy                          cost   accuracy  escalated   vs baseline   cost saved
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
cascade-s>l@0.91               3732¢      86.2%        78%      +2.5 pts         +12%
value-raw@0.1800               2226¢      82.6%        66%      +6.4 pts         +37%
value-cal@0.1800               2281¢      83.6%        73%      +7.1 pts         +39%

Pricing the decision is worth +3.9 points and 25% of the bill over the
same policy with a fixed threshold. Same models, same confidence signal,
same escalation target - the only change is dividing by what the escalation
costs. That is the largest single improvement in this report.

Now the calibration question, honestly. Best operating point says
recalibration is worth +0.7 points here. Best-point comparisons reward
whichever variant got luckier on the sweep grid, so check the whole sweep:

  raw signal,    16 lambdas: mean lift +3.45 pts
  scaled signal, 16 lambdas: mean lift +3.53 pts
  scaled beats raw at 7 of 16 matched lambdas

So: real, but small and not robust. The honest conclusion is that
recalibration is worth EXACTLY zero to the threshold rule - proved above,
48 of 48 threshold pairs identical on all 4,000 queries - and worth a
fraction of a point to the value rule, well inside the noise of choosing
lambda. The reason is visible in section 1's histogram: the distortion is
worst at the extremes, where 66% of the mass sits and where the decision is
unambiguous either way. Fixing a number that was never close to the line
changes nothing.

The general lesson is the one worth carrying: whether calibration matters
is a property of how the decision rule CONSUMES the signal, not of the
signal. Nobody can answer "should we invest in calibration" without first
knowing whether anything downstream does arithmetic on the number - and if
it does, whether the queries near the decision boundary are the distorted
ones. Two questions, both answerable in an afternoon, and neither is the
one that gets asked.

6. THE FAILURE MODE NOBODY DESIGNS FOR: IT WORKS
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A cost-optimising router concentrates traffic on whichever model answers
well. Then that provider degrades and every request retries into a timeout.
The routing policy is correct throughout and the service is down.

4000 requests: 2842 served, 745 failed over to mid, 1109 degraded to small on
budget, 49 rejected. Every request accounted for: true

acme burns its cap and is then served on the small model instead of being
rejected. That degraded count is the number to alert on: a customer is
silently getting a worse product and nothing in the error rate will show it.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SUMMARY
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. The cheap model's confidence RANKS well (AUC 0.918) and LIES about its
   numbers (ECE 0.059, worst bin 0.226). Different failures, different fixes,
   and only one of them can possibly matter to a threshold rule.
2. Against the usual chord baseline, cascade-small>large gains +6.4 points.
   Against the fleet's convex hull - which includes just using the mid model
   - it gains +2.5. Choosing the baseline changed the verdict more than any
   algorithm in this report.
3. Temperature scaling cut calibration error 51% and moved the threshold
   cascade's frontier by exactly 0.0 points: 48 of 48 threshold pairs made
   identical decisions on all 4000 queries. A monotone transform cannot change
   a threshold's decision set. It makes the knob interpretable, which is
   worth doing and is not a quality win.
4. What DID move the frontier was dividing by the cost of the escalation:
   +2.5 -> +6.4 points and 12% -> 37% of the bill, from one division.
   Recalibrating that same rule added a further +0.7 at its best lambda and
   +0.08 averaged over the sweep - real, but inside the noise.
5. Best real router: value-cal@0.1800 at 83.6% accuracy for 51% of the always-large
   bill, 39% cheaper than the zero-information baseline at matched accuracy,
   capturing 59% of the headroom between that baseline and the oracle.

Reproduce: go run ./cmd/router -seed 20240612 -n 8000
